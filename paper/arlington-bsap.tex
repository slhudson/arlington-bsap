\section{Introduction}

Arlington has elected a county board since 1870. This report sets out what the
record shows about that Board's size and about the people who have sat on it,
and it names the themes the County could take up next. It recommends no form of
government, and it does not argue that any history compels any reform. Where the
record supports a reading, the report shows the evidence; where the record runs
out, it says so.

Five topics follow, each with the figures that carry it and a timeline of the
events it turns on: the Board's size against the County's population, gender,
race and ethnicity, where members have lived, and age. A sixth section sets out
two lines of analysis begun and left unfinished, with the history of local legal
authority, and asks whether they belong in this report. A seventh names six
themes the Board could take up next.

\section{Board Size}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{residents_per_seat.pdf}
  \caption{Arlington County residents and residents per Board seat, 1870--2020,
  with a dashed rule at 1932, when the Board went from three seats to five.
  Both series count people, so one axis serves both. The denominator is the
  seats that exist --- three through 1930, five after --- not the members
  serving, so a vacancy does not move the line.}
  \label{fig:residents-per-seat}
\end{figure}

The Board has had five seats since 1932, and the county has outgrown that
number several times over. In 1930 each of three seats stood for 8,872
residents, up from 1,062 in 1870; the expansion to five arrived as the county
was about to grow fivefold in twenty years, from 26,615 residents in 1930 to
135,449 in 1950. Each seat now stands for 47,729 residents, and the Board has
not changed size to meet it.

Figure~\ref{fig:residents-per-seat} carries no benchmark line. The cube-root
law describes national parliaments and sets no standard for a county; at
Arlington's 2020 population it would imply a Board of 62. Peer localities make
a fairer yardstick. Among Virginia's cities and the fourteen counties set beside
Arlington, only Fairfax, Chesterfield, Henrico and Prince William stand for more
residents per member, and Loudoun and Virginia Beach stand for about as many.
Among eighty southeastern governing bodies of 150,000 to 300,000 residents,
five counties of five seats or fewer stand for more.
% The peer figures (localities_residents, localities_density and
% localities_southeastern) are built but not placed in this paper yet.

\timeline{
  1847--1870 & After retrocession a county court of justices of the peace runs
  the county, doing the administrative work alongside the judicial. Its justices
  are elected by district from 1852, but there is no elected
  board.\autocite[art.~VI, secs.~25--27]{vaconstitution1851} \\[2pt]
  1870--1931 & Virginia's 1869 constitution creates the boards of supervisors
  itself: the supervisors elected in each township constitute the county's
  board.\autocite[art.~VII, sec.~2]{vaconstitution1869} Three units --- Arlington,
  Jefferson and Washington, townships until 1875 and magisterial districts after
  --- elect one supervisor each. \\[2pt]
  1930--1932 & Arlington adopts the County Manager Plan by referendum in 1930
  and elects its first at-large Board in November 1931. The Board that sits in
  January 1932 has five seats in place of three, is elected at large, and is no
  longer a board of supervisors.\autocite{samuel2026} \\[2pt]
  1930--1950 & The county grows from 26,615 residents to 135,449, with the Board
  at five seats. \\
}

The series begins in 1870 because Arlington elected no governing board before
then. The county court did the administrative work the Board took up --- the
Code of the period gives the county levy to the court\autocite[ch.~53, secs.~1
and 3]{vacode1860} --- and neither that Code nor the 1851 constitution names
Alexandria County. Both apply to every county in the state, so the court's role
here follows from the general law.

Virginia's 1869 constitution created the boards of supervisors itself, without
an enabling act, but three acts of the 1869--70 session stood Arlington's Board
up in fact: they laid the counties off into townships, provided for a supervisor
elected in each, and gave the board a name it could sue
by.\autocite[chs.~39, 76 and 188]{vaacts1870} The roster therefore begins at
the May 1870 election, not at ratification. When magisterial districts replaced
the townships in 1875, the same three units took the new name, so the seat count
did not move.\autocite[ch.~76, sec.~1]{vaacts1875}

The 1930--1932 row carries three changes that arrive together: five seats in
place of three, at-large election in place of districts, and members who are no
longer supervisors. The County Attorney's July 2026 report observes that the
County Manager Plan appears to be the only county form whose members are not
called supervisors, and the Code requires at-large election of all of them under
no other form.\autocite{samuel2026}

\section{Gender}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{members_by_gender.pdf}
  \caption{Arlington County Board seats by gender, 1870--2026, in seats, not
  shares. The dashed rule marks 1932, when the Board went from three seats to
  five and magisterial districts gave way to at-large election; the first woman
  took a seat that year. Census records identify gender for
  \genderCensusShareMembers{} per cent of all members and the press's pronouns
  and honorifics for the rest but two: William H. Robinson (1877) and Walter G.
  Willson (1889) rest on the default, man. Fractional seat values arise where a
  member served part of a year.}
  \label{fig:board-gender}
\end{figure}

Women have held about a quarter of all seat-years since 1932 and have never
held a majority for more than a single year, three of the five seats in 1983
and again in 2024. The first woman took a seat in 1932, and the longest
stretch with no woman at all, 1959 to 1973, came a generation after her.

\timeline{
  1932 & The first woman takes a seat in the year the Board expands to five and
  moves to at-large election. \\[2pt]
  1959--1973 & Fifteen consecutive years with no woman on the Board. \\[2pt]
  1983, 2024 & The only two years in which women hold a majority of the
  seats. \\
}

Three things change in 1932 --- five seats, at-large election, and the first
woman --- and no figure built from this record can separate them. Contemporaries
drew a link between structure and women's chances: in 1938 the Arlington County
Woman's Democratic Club opposed staggered terms on the ground that they would
make it ``virtually impossible for a woman to be elected to the
board.''\autocite{sun1938referendum}

Gender rests on two kinds of evidence. A census sheet names it for members
through the 1960s; the 1950 census is the last open, and the 1960 census closes
for seventy-two years. For every other member the source is the press: the
``she'' or ``Ms.'' a contemporary article uses, quoted and cited member by
member. Race has a different footing: the Board's modern members of color are
named in published sources, but every other member is recorded as White by
default, so the default carries more of the race figure than it does of this one.

\section{Race and Ethnicity}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{residents_by_race.pdf}
  \caption{Arlington County residents by race and ethnicity, 1870--2020. Panel
  (a) gives counts, panel (b) each group's share. A line in (a) begins the year
  the Census first counted that group. The dashed rule marks 1980, the first
  census to ask Hispanic origin of every household; from there the categories
  are the Census Bureau's own race-by-Hispanic-origin crosstabs, so nobody is
  counted twice. The White line leaves the top of panel (a) at 50,000 in the
  late 1930s, where the triangle marks it, and keeps climbing; it reaches
  161,329 by 1970. Plotting its full range would flatten the other four series.}
  \label{fig:residents-race}
\end{figure}

Arlington was a majority-Black county until 1890. Between 1870 and 1950 the
Black share of the county fell from 63 per cent to 4.8 per cent --- while the
Black population itself grew over the same period. The collapse is arithmetic,
not departure: the denominator grew. Since 1980 the county has diversified
largely through Hispanic and Asian American and Pacific Islander growth.

A rising county count does not rule out displacement. A neighborhood can be
cleared while the county total grows, because in-migration elsewhere masks the
loss, and county totals cannot tell the two apart. Bestebreurtje's dissertation
on African American community development in Arlington is the source on that
question, and nobody has yet read it
through.\autocite{bestebreurtje2017}

\timeline{
  1870--1890 & A majority-Black county. The Board is created in 1870, and the
  census counts Freedman's Village --- the settlement of formerly enslaved
  people on the Custis-Lee estate --- beside the three townships. \\[2pt]
  1890--1950 & White in-migration collapses the Black share from 63 per cent to
  under 5: streetcar suburbs, then federal growth. The Black population itself
  grows over the same period. \\[2pt]
  1980--present & Hispanic and Asian growth, from the first census that asks. \\
}

Two gaps are worth naming. No source read for this report gives a closure date
for Freedman's Village, and none is asserted here. What the settlement was, how
many people lived in it, and what became of it are the questions local
knowledge is most likely to answer.

The figure also shows almost nothing moving between 1950 and 1980. The Black
share is already low by 1950, and the census has not yet asked the Hispanic and
Asian American questions. If something was happening in those thirty years, no
source read for this report has shown it.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{members_by_race.pdf}
  \caption{Arlington County Board seats by race and ethnicity, 1871--2026, in
  seats, not shares, on the same scale as
  Figure~\ref{fig:board-gender}. The dashed rule marks 1932. US census records
  identify race for the five members seated before 1890; published research by
  the Arlington Historical Society and the Center for Local History identifies
  the members of color seated since 1988. About \raceAssumedShareMembers{} per
  cent of all members are recorded as White because no source confirms it. No
  Asian American or Pacific Islander member has served in any year, so no such
  band is drawn.}
  \label{fig:board-race}
\end{figure}

For two decades after the Board was created, Black members held one or two of
its three seats almost continuously. Then no member of color held a seat for
four decades, from 1889 to the at-large referendum, with the district system
still intact and Black residents still close to half the county. Since members
of color returned in 1988, never more than one has served at a time, and no
Asian American or Pacific Islander member has served at all.

That four-decade break is the finding, and it does not fall in 1930. It opens
in 1889, under the district system, four decades before at-large election
arrives. At-large election therefore looks like what prevented a reversal, not
what caused the exclusion.

One default runs underneath the figure: every member across 156 years who is
not named below as a member of color is recorded as White because no source says
otherwise. For the century between 1889 and 1987 that default
rests on the Arlington Historical Society's own statement that William T.
Newman, Jr. was the first African American elected to the Board since
Reconstruction. Nobody has checked that statement against a roster.

\timeline{
  \multicolumn{2}{@{}l}{\textit{1871--1888 --- Black representation under the
  district system}} \\[4pt]
  \multicolumn{2}{@{}l}{\textit{1889--1919 --- Suppressing Black
  representation}} \\[2pt]
  1888 & A sitting Black member removed on residence grounds. \\[2pt]
  1894 & The Walton Act makes the ballot official and bars any other as a
  legal vote; a voter must cross out the name of every candidate he does not
  want, with a mark reaching three-fourths of the name's length or the stroke
  does not count, and an office left with more than one name standing is
  voided outright. Only the electoral board's own appointed constable, not a
  judge, may read or mark a ballot for a voter who cannot do it himself, and
  handing out a sample ballot is a crime. The act demands the literacy a test
  would, without naming one.\autocite{waltonact1894} \\[2pt]
  1901--02 & The Virginia Constitutional Convention, which writes
  disenfranchisement into the constitution. \\[4pt]
  \multicolumn{2}{@{}l}{\textit{1920--1940 --- Replacing district
  representation with countywide majority contests}} \\[2pt]
  1922 & \textit{Bennett v. Garrett}: the Supreme Court of Appeals rejects
  Clarendon's petition to incorporate as a town, holding that where corporate
  limits are carved out of a thickly settled section the ``general good of the
  community'' the statute protects is the whole county's.
  \autocite{bennettvgarrett1922} \\[2pt]
  1930 & At-large elections replace magisterial districts, changing who the
  constituency is. \\[2pt]
  1938 & Staggered terms, approved 1,539 to 1,487, so most elections fill a
  single seat --- changing the threshold, not the
  constituency.\autocite[11]{arlingtonelections2021} \\
}

Two mechanisms are at work in that periodization, not one. At-large election
changes who the constituency is: every member is elected countywide, so a group
concentrated in one part of the county cannot elect anyone from it. Staggering
changes the threshold: one seat at a time means every contest requires a
countywide majority. Staggering is what makes at-large binding, and no reform
effort since has gone after it.

\textit{Bennett v. Garrett} sits under the 1920--1940 era rather than on its
own because its reasoning --- Arlington is one community, and Clarendon only
``a part of a thickly settled community'' inside it --- is the argument that
carries into countywide election eight years later. It appears in the County
Attorney's report as a citation, not as history.\autocite[6, n.~7]{samuel2026}

The phrase often repeated for that holding --- ``continuous, contiguous, and
homogeneous community'' --- is not the court's. It belongs to Cornelia Rose,
who puts it in quotation marks with no source given in her 1976 county
history and reuses it twice more, for the 1932 change to at-large election
and for how much less homogeneous the county had become by 1976; the words
appear nowhere in the opinion itself.\autocite[176, 197, 229]{rose1976}

\timeline{
  \multicolumn{2}{@{}l}{\textit{1939--1987 --- No Black member serves}} \\[2pt]
  1947, 1956 & The General Assembly authorizes Arlington alone to elect its
  school board, then repeals that authority four months after the board votes
  to integrate. \\[2pt]
  1965 & The Voting Rights Act; Virginia becomes a covered jurisdiction subject
  to preclearance. \\[2pt]
  1974--75 & \textit{Vollin v. Kimbel}: eight Arlington residents sue, arguing
  that at-large election dilutes Black voting strength; the district court
  dismisses it in 1974 and the Fourth Circuit affirms in
  1975.\autocite{vollin1974} \\[2pt]
  1976 & Over two hundred voters petition for a referendum on district
  election; the Virginia Supreme Court denies it, holding that the change must
  come from Richmond.\autocite{vollinelectoralboard} \\[2pt]
  1980, 1986 & \textit{Bolden} requires proof of discriminatory intent;
  \textit{Gingles} replaces it with a test of effects. \\[4pt]
  \multicolumn{2}{@{}l}{\textit{1988--present --- Members of color return, one
  at a time}} \\[2pt]
  1988-- & Newman, Monroe, Tejada, Dorsey, Spain. \\[2pt]
  1992 & Elected school boards return statewide, 45 years after Arlington was
  first authorized and 36 after the authority was taken away. \\
}

These two eras explain less than the first three do. That no Black member served
for half a century is a fact about the record; the causes overlap, and this
report can show them without ranking them. The school board rows sit here rather
than with the legal-authority material because the 1956 repeal is about race and
about elections at once.

The two Vollin actions are separate cases. The 1974 federal suit brought the
vote-dilution claim, and it failed. The 1976 state case asked who may change the
electoral structure at all, and its answer --- Richmond --- is why local legal
authority appears in this report's scope section.

What these legal changes did in Arlington specifically is not established.
Preclearance applied to Virginia from 1965, but nobody has yet determined what
it covered in Arlington. That is a research question, not a finding.

\section{Neighborhood}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{members_residence_coverage.pdf}
  \caption{How precisely each Board seat's occupant can be located, 1870--2026.
  Each year averages the months the Board existed, so a vacancy leaves a gap
  and a member who served part of a year fills a fraction of a seat. The shade gives the finest geographic unit at
  which the person sitting in it can be placed: darkest a street address,
  palest a magisterial district or a side of the County, grey no location at
  all. Census records carry street addresses through the mid-twentieth century.
  Before 1932 a member is placed by his census sheet, which outside the towns
  names a magisterial district and no street. More recent members are located
  largely by neighborhood mentions in the press and in public profiles.}
  \label{fig:board-residence}
\end{figure}

The record runs backwards: it is best in the middle. From the mid-1930s to the
late 1990s nearly every seat has a street address, because census sheets and
city directories carried them. Those sources then end --- the census closes for
seventy-two years and the directories stop being published --- and nothing
public replaces them. Since 2000 the best available unit is a neighborhood
drawn from press coverage.

Before 1932 only the census places a member, and outside the towns its sheets
name a magisterial district and leave the street column blank. Thirty-four of
the fifty-three residence claims from those years reach no closer than a
district, and the county had only three of them.

The seat a man held narrows nothing further. Until 1903 no law required a
supervisor to live in the district he represented, so the Board's minute books
--- which name all three seats in every year from 1870 --- establish who sat
without establishing where he lived.

That gap is what the request below asks about: two of the five seats have no
location at all for much of 1972 to 2012. Those are years the modern County
documented for itself, and they are where outside sources run out. One caveat
applies throughout: many of the addresses held are dated after the member left
office, and an obituary address is where someone died, not where they lived when
elected.

\subsection*{Request: County-held residency records}

The County may hold internal records identifying where Board members lived,
including payroll and personnel records, candidate filings and oaths of office,
and voter registration records. If the Board would like member residency
examined in this report, could the County share these data --- if necessary at
an aggregated level, such as neighborhood, zip code or precinct?

What is legally releasable is the constraint, and the County knows that ground.
Units that would serve, coarsest first, are zip code, precinct, civic
association and census tract; tract is the only one that joins to census
demographics, but any of them supports the clustering question.

\section{Age}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{residents_by_age.pdf}
  \caption{Arlington residents by age band as a share of the county, at each
  census from 1910 to 2020, shaded light to dark from youngest to oldest. 1910
  and 1920 are counted from the full-count schedules; from 1930, the published
  county age tables. US decennial census, 1910--2020.}
  \label{fig:residents-age}
\end{figure}

This is context for the Board's ages, not a comparison with them. There is no
defensible optimal age for a Board member, and drawing the two on one panel
would imply that there is.

The county grew by more than half over these forty years, from 152,599
residents to 238,643, and its age structure barely moved. The 25-to-34 band is
the largest at every census, between 24 and 28 per cent: Arlington has been a
young-adult county for as long as the census can measure it. The 65-and-over
share fell from 11.6 per cent in 1980 to 8.7 per cent in 2010, recovering only
to 10.6 per cent in 2020. The 18-to-24 band fell from 13.9 to 9.9 per cent while
the 35-to-44 band rose from 12.6 to 16.1. The under-18 share is flat, 16.4 to
17.6 per cent.

The stability is what makes the next figure readable. If the county's age
structure does not move, then movement in the Board's age range is about the
Board, not about who lives in Arlington.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{members_age.pdf}
  \caption{Ages of the Arlington County Board, 1870--2026. Each diagonal is one
  member, running from their age at first election to their age at departure;
  it rises at 45 degrees because everyone ages a year per year. The band behind
  runs from the youngest to the oldest member sitting in that year, so its
  height is the age range on the Board at that moment. Birth years come from
  census records for members seated before 1960 and from obituaries and
  published profiles after that; exact birth dates are rarely known, so age is
  approximate within one year. A year is drawn only where all but at most one
  sitting member has a birth year.}
  \label{fig:board-age}
\end{figure}

From 2008 to 2011 the Board sat inside the narrowest age span in the series:
five people between 49 and 56. In 1996--97 the ceiling drops from 72 to about 42
in two years. Part of the widening after 1932 is mechanical, because five people
drawn from the same distribution spread wider than three do.

The band is drawn from the youngest to the oldest sitting member, not as
quartiles. When the oldest member leaves and nobody older replaces them, the
drop in the ceiling is the finding, and quartiles would hide it.

\subsection*{Request: two birth years}

Birth year records have not been found for two of the 75 members seated since
1932: B. M. Smith (1933--1935) and Susan R. Cunningham (2024--present).

Cunningham matters more of the two, because she sits at the end of the series.
A Yale graduation year is the only evidence held, and her age in
Figure~\ref{fig:board-age} is inferred from it. She is serving, so the birth
year is hers to give, and the Board office could ask her directly. The
candidate coverage, the county and campaign biographies, the questionnaires and
VOTE411 all print no age. Smith is archival, not personal: the Evening Star has
been read across his term, and the 1930 and 1940 censuses searched under
variant spellings. County personnel or payroll records would most likely carry
him, which is the request the neighborhood section already makes.

The figure drops any year in which more than one sitting member's age is
unknown, so a single missing value can blank the most recent years.

\section{Project Scope}

Two lines of analysis have been begun and not finished. Neither sits obviously
inside the equity resolution, so this report asks rather than assumes.

\textbf{Turnout} --- who actually elects the Board, and how that has changed.
Board elections draw a smaller and a different electorate than presidential
ones. Registration records before 2010 would be needed; the published series do
not go back further.

\textbf{Partisanship} --- the hardest thing on the list to measure defensibly.
Twenty-three terms between 1932 and 1960 carry no party or coalition label
anywhere, and before 1932 none were recorded.

\subsection*{Local legal authority}

The County Attorney's July 2026 report covers the law thoroughly and contains
no history.\autocite{samuel2026} The history is what this report could add, if
the County wants it. None of the seven episodes below is about elections:

\timeline{
  1882 & \textit{Kirkham v. Russell}: refusing mandamus to seven men whom
  Petersburg's outgoing common council had elected to city offices three days
  before its term ended, the Supreme Court of Appeals quotes Dillon on
  Municipal Corporations for the rule that a charter grants only the powers
  its words comprehend or necessarily imply.\autocite{kirkhamvrussell1882}
  \\[2pt]
  1896 & \textit{City of Winchester v. Redmond}: refusing to pay a reward
  Winchester's council had promised for catching incendiaries, the court
  quotes Dillon directly for the rule that now carries his name --- a
  municipality holds only the powers granted in express words, those
  necessarily implied in them, and those indispensable to its declared
  purposes. Virginia's Dillon's Rule is judicial, never
  legislated.\autocite{winchestervredmond1896} \\[2pt]
  1958--59 & The Commission on Incorporation takes up whether Arlington should
  become a city, advised by the University of Virginia's Bureau of Public
  Administration. \\[2pt]
  1968--71 & Virginia's constitutional revision commission proposes letting a
  charter county or city act on anything state law does not deny it; the
  General Assembly strikes the provision before the new constitution reaches
  voters. The opposition is the Virginia Municipal League's --- ``somewhat
  counterintuitive'', Schragger and Retzloff call it, since the Virginia
  Association of Counties had proposed the opposite in 1968 and reverses only
  in its 2020 legislative program.\autocite[200]{schragger2022} \\[2pt]
  1971 & The General Assembly conforms the County Manager Plan's statutory
  text to the new Constitution: the sections setting the county board's
  composition, the county manager's powers, and the board's authority to
  abolish offices each swap a cross-reference to the 1902 Constitution for
  the equivalent provision of the 1971 Constitution, moving no
  authority.\autocite{vaacts1971exsess} \\[2pt]
  1977 & \textit{Commonwealth v. Arlington County Board}: the Virginia Supreme
  Court voids the County's collective bargaining agreements with its own
  employees, and bars public-sector bargaining
  statewide.\autocite{commonwealthvarlington1977} \\[2pt]
  1985 & \textit{County Board of Arlington v. Brown}: the Board votes to lease
  the courthouse parking lot to a private developer for seventy-five years and
  directs its County Manager to sign, he refuses, and the Virginia Supreme
  Court holds that the County never had the power to grant the
  lease.\autocite{brownvarlington1985} \\[2pt]
  2000 & \textit{Arlington County v. White}: the County extends employee health
  benefits to domestic partners, and the Virginia Supreme Court holds that it
  had no authority to do so.\autocite{arlingtonvwhite2000} \\
}

Kirkham and Winchester made Dillon's Rule Virginia law; the County Attorney's
report lists three further decisions under it, and all three are Arlington's
own. The 1977 decision settled public-sector bargaining for the whole state,
and the 2000 decision settled how far a locality may go on employee benefits.
The 1985 decision is the one a study of the form of government wants, because
the official the Board could not move was its own Manager: it had approved
the lease and ordered him to sign, and the court told him he had been right
to refuse. None of the three is about elections, and all turn on the question
this report asks --- what Arlington may decide for itself. Nor could
Arlington ask Richmond to change the rule on its own terms: the 1968--71
reform that would have let a charter county or city act without the General
Assembly's leave failed, and the sharpest statement of the same constraint is
the 1976 Vollin holding in the race section above, where Arlington's voters
could not put district election to a referendum because the change had to
come from Richmond.

\section{Themes for Future Work}

This report's five topics and the legal history above point toward six themes
the Board may want to take up next. Naming them is not a recommendation on
any of them.

\textbf{Board duties} --- how authority divides between the elected Board, the
County Manager, and County staff. The 1985 \textit{County Board of Arlington
v. Brown} decision in the scope section above is this report's sharpest
evidence on the question: the Board approved a lease and ordered its own
Manager to sign it, and the Virginia Supreme Court held that he had been
right to refuse. The 1930--1932 adoption of the County Manager Plan is the
origin of the arrangement that case tested. No Virginia appellate court has
revisited Board-Manager authority under the Plan since. The one later
decision this report finds on a comparable non-interference clause is a
circuit court's: \textit{City of Richmond City Council v. Wilder} (2007)
reads Richmond's mayor-council charter the other way, holding that its
clause giving the Chief Administrative Officer ``ultimate responsibility''
for personnel reaches administrative and executive employees only, so the
Council itself kept the power to appoint the legislative staff who serve
it.\autocite{richmondcouncilvwilder2007} Richmond's charter is not the
County Manager Plan, and the ruling was never
appealed, so it settles nothing for Arlington; it stands only as the
nearest Virginia court has come to the question since Brown.

\textbf{Board structure} --- the number of seats, whether the chair is
directly elected, and how terms are set and sequenced. The Board has not
changed size since 1932; Figure~\ref{fig:residents-per-seat} is this report's
evidence for what that holds fixed while the county around it keeps growing,
and the report takes no position on what, if anything, should follow from it.

\textbf{Voting systems} --- at-large election against district election,
single-member against multi-member districts, and ranked choice voting
against plurality rules, each weighed for what it does to representation.
The race section above is this report's most developed evidence on the
question: at-large election and staggered terms are two separable
mechanisms, and the four-decade break in Black representation opens under
the district system, before either reform arrives.

\textbf{Civic engagement and public participation} --- the commissions,
advisory groups, public comment rules, meeting requirements, and records
access through which residents reach their government, and where gaps in
that access fall. This report does not examine any of it directly. The
neighborhood section above is the closest it comes: where Board members
themselves have lived is hardest to know for exactly the years, 1972 to
2012, that the County's own records should document best.

\textbf{Legal constraints} --- what Virginia law lets Arlington decide for
itself. Dillon's Rule, the three decisions that have tested it against
Arlington specifically, and the 1968--71 failure to loosen it statewide are
all in the scope section above; so is the 1976 \textit{Vollin} holding, which
closes the race section on the same point: Arlington's voters could not put
district election to a referendum, because the change had to come from
Richmond.

\textbf{Comparative governance} --- how Arlington's Board compares to peer
Virginia localities and to jurisdictions elsewhere. The scope section above
asks whether to make that comparison at all; the figures exist and are held
back until the Board answers.

These six are not independent of each other: a question about Board
structure is also a question about voting systems, and a change to either
runs into the legal constraints above. The report raises them as what its
own findings point toward, not as a sequence or a priority among them.

\section*{Notes on the figures}

\subsection*{Palette}

A figure's palette follows from whether its categories are ordered. Ordered
categories --- the grades of residence precision in
Figure~\ref{fig:board-residence}, the age bands in
Figure~\ref{fig:residents-age} --- take one sequential ramp, so that adjacent
bands read as adjacent and the darker end is more of the thing. Unordered
categories --- race, gender --- take the Okabe--Ito palette, where no hue
implies a rank, with a near-neutral for the largest group and a grey for
residuals. No figure defines a ramp of its own. \texttt{docs/figures.md} holds
the detail and the departures from the Urban Institute style guide this project
otherwise follows.

\subsection*{Board size and population}

Population is the decennial census count for the territory the Board governed
in each year. Alexandria city has been independent since 1871 and is reported
separately from 1900; Alexandria annexed 866 acres in 1915 and a further tract
in 1930, so part of the movement between the 1910 and 1930 censuses is
territory changing hands, not people arriving or leaving.

Seats in Figure~\ref{fig:residents-per-seat} are counted as authorized, not
as filled --- three through 1930, five after --- so a vacancy does not move
the line.

\subsection*{Seat-years}

Figures~\ref{fig:board-gender}, \ref{fig:board-race},
\ref{fig:board-residence} and \ref{fig:board-age} count the Board in
seat-years: a member who sat four months of a year counts four twelfths of a
seat, so a year in which one member replaced another shows as half a seat each
rather than a whole seat for both. The denominator is the months the Board
existed, which is twelve in every year but its first --- Arlington's Board came
into existence at the May 1870 election, so 1870 counts eight months.

Two years fall below a full Board and both are real: 1873, when a district sat
vacant from June to November, and 1990, between a February resignation and a
May special election. 1931 is not reported in the source and is drawn as a gap,
not a zero.

\subsection*{Gender and race}

Race and gender are two splits of the same seats, drawn on the same scale, so
Figures~\ref{fig:board-gender} and \ref{fig:board-race} can be read against
each other. Board members carry one race code each, so the Board categories do
not overlap and can be read against the census ones directly: a Hispanic or
Latino member appears in that category whatever their race.

From 1980 the groups in Figure~\ref{fig:residents-race} are the Census Bureau's
race categories crossed with Hispanic origin. They do not overlap and account
for the county exactly: Hispanic or Latino is of any race, and every other
group is non-Hispanic. Before 1980 they are race categories as reported,
because the Bureau did not ask Hispanic origin of every household until then.
Race and Hispanic origin are separate questions answered by the same people, so
those earlier categories can overlap: in 1970 they sum to about 2.8 per cent
more than the county, and panel (b) rescales that column to 100 per cent.

Hispanic or Latino residents were not counted separately before 1970, nor Asian
and Pacific Islander residents before 1950. Each was small when first counted
--- 1,387 and 57 residents, 0.8 and 0.04 per cent of the county --- so little
is likely to be hidden, though Hispanic residents before 1970 were recorded as
white rather than not recorded at all. Asian and Pacific Islander are reported
together before 2000 and separately after, and are combined throughout so the
series is consistent. ``Other or Multiracial'' is American Indian and Alaska
Native, some other race, and two or more races; before 1980 it is the residents
the four reported categories do not account for, never more than 202 people.

Terms follow the Bureau's own wording throughout.

\subsection*{Neighborhood}

A place is counted in Figure~\ref{fig:board-residence} whenever it is dated, so
a member whose only recorded place comes from after their service still counts
at that precision. A magisterial district and a side of the County are the same
grade of knowledge, told apart only by the era of the source, and read as one
shade.

\subsection*{Age}

Figure~\ref{fig:board-age} runs the Board's full span, 1870 to the present.
Before 1932 the Board has three seats, and five members in that stretch have
no birth year found in any source, so the band is fragments there rather than
one continuous ribbon.
