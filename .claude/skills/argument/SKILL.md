# Argument

A report section is not just a container for true facts about its subject.
It argues something, usually without stating a thesis sentence, and every
paragraph in it either carries that argument or doesn't. This skill is
about finding the claim, checking it against the evidence actually held,
and sorting what belongs from what doesn't — before worrying about how any
of it is phrased.

## How to work this with Sally

Conversational, not a drafting pass. Read the section as it stands (or the
proposal it's rewritten from) and state its argument back to her in plain
words before asking anything. Then go one question at a time, in bullets,
plain language, no project idiom, and wait for her answer before the next.
Label pieces with letters/numbers only once she's asked for that — don't
pre-impose a numbering scheme on a section that hasn't been discussed yet.

If a proposed claim turns on an empirical pattern, check the data before
either side commits to it. A scratch plot answers faster than an argument
does, and a claim that only *sounds* right from the mechanism shouldn't
survive contact with a one-afternoon look at what's actually in
`data/clean/`. Save a reusable script, not just a one-off chart, in case
the question comes back.

Log every decision as you go — a short memo, not the conversation
transcript — so there's something to hand to a drafting thread without
replaying the back-and-forth that got there. Don't edit `paper/` or
`docs/` during this kind of conversation unless asked; the output is the
memo and a model recommendation for whoever drafts from it.

## Shapes to recognise in a section that isn't holding together

Found by walking Election Method's draft against its own claim before
writing a word of it (5 October 2026). Each is a shape to recognise, not a
checklist to run once, and not specific to that section.

**A definition that only comes true at one point in history isn't a
definition.** Election Method's draft explained, as part of defining
block voting in general, that a majority bloc can also win a whole board
one seat at a time across several single-winner elections — but that
pattern only exists once staggering has actually happened. A definitions
section states what a mechanism *is*, independent of when anything in the
subject's history used it that way; a pattern that is only true starting in
a particular year belongs in that year's subsection, not in the definitions
block it got borrowed into.

**A hedge is a sign two cases got glued together.** "Hyper-majoritarian in
a narrow sense, one that describes what the rule permits and not what any
election produced" was straining to cover both a multi-seat sweep and a
one-seat-at-a-time majority in one paragraph. Where a sentence needs
"in a sense" or "narrowly" to stay true, look for the two cases underneath
it and give each its own sentence, or its own subsection.

**"What a rule permits" and "what this history's evidence shows" are
different claims and need different support.** The nominating-stage
argument read as plausible because it described what *could* happen under
a one-party nominating stage. What the project's own data showed — 20 of 89
years recorded, and the one clean test case won the general for an
independent, not the party's nominee — did not support it. Before a
section states what a mechanism produces in this subject's actual history,
look at the data that would test it, even roughly; a plausible mechanism
and a supported claim are not the same thing, and a thin or contrary
dataset means the claim isn't ready to print, not that it needs stronger
wording.

**Content belongs with the section whose claim it supports, not the
section whose decade it shares.** A paragraph and figure about the
electorate shrinking (the Walton Act, the poll tax) sat in Election Method
because it happened in the same years as the district system — but it's
about who could vote, and Election Method is about how votes become seats.
It moved to Race and Ethnicity, which already covers franchise. Two
passages about the same years are not necessarily part of the same
argument.

**A subsection's date range should track the rule, not an era label.**
"At Large, 1930-1938" implied at-large election ended in 1938; it didn't —
staggering that year only changed how many seats were on each ballot. Date
a subsection by how long the thing it's about was actually true.

**Motive and mechanism are different kinds of material.** The county's own
legal and cultural reasoning for adopting a method (Bennett v. Garrett, the
"continuous, contiguous, and homogeneous" phrase) answers why the method
was chosen; it doesn't describe what the method does. Keep both if both
earn their place, but keep them visibly separate — motive as the section's
opening frame, mechanism as its substance.

**When a new rule arrives at the end of a history, the job isn't to narrate
its adoption — it's to test the section's claim against it.** Ranked choice
voting is the newest method Election Method covers. Simply describing when
it arrived and how it's been used so far leaves the section's own argument
untested against its own last case. State directly whether the new rule
changes the pattern the section just built, and where it doesn't.

## After the argument is settled

Hand off to the `prose` skill for how to write it — voice, sentence shape,
the project's register. That skill assumes the claim and structure are
